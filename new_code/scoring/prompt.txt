You are scoring one participant's written recall for a memory study.

The participant read a short text and then wrote down as much of it as they could remember. The text they read was either four original paragraphs or another participant's recall of them, so their recall may be partial, reordered, blended across paragraphs, or wrong in places. Your job is to decide, for each of 56 propositions from the four original paragraphs, whether this recall contains it.

HOW PROPOSITIONS ARE WRITTEN

Each proposition is given two ways:
- the notation, in the form PREDICATE, ARGUMENT, ARGUMENT (Kintsch, 1974). This says what the recall has to contain.
- plain words, which say which part of the story the proposition is about. Two propositions can share the same notation: FOR, DIRECTIONS appears twice, once for Nancy asking the man and once for the man asking the driver, and the plain words tell them apart. Beyond picking out the part of the story, go by the notation. If the plain words mention a person or detail the notation leaves out, the recall does not need it.

Some arguments refer to another proposition by its id, written in brackets. For example, PROMISE, PROFESSOR, NANCY, [gossip_7] means "the professor promised Nancy [gossip_7]", where gossip_7 is "LEAVE, PROFESSOR, WIFE".

RULES

1. Paraphrase counts. Accept any plausible synonym or paraphrase that generally means the same thing, judged by what the words mean in the recall's own context.
2. Credit only what the recall states, in any wording. Do not credit what a reader would have to work out from the rest of the recall, even when it seems obvious, and never fill anything in from the original paragraphs.
3. Score each proposition only on what it says itself. Other propositions carry the rest. A proposition about the affair and the professor does not also need Nancy, because Nancy has her own proposition. But when the notation names a character, the recall has to refer to that character. "Someone" is not Nancy.
4. A proposition stated wrongly is absent: the wrong person, the wrong thing, or the opposite of what the original says.
5. Look through the whole recall for every proposition. Content can appear anywhere, in any order, and mixed in with other paragraphs.
6. Ignore tense, spelling and grammar, as long as the meaning is understandable. A recall that hedges or is phrased as a question ("I think...", "Nancy is having an affair??") still states it.
7. Some propositions add a detail to another one, such as FOR, DIRECTIONS; ABOUT, AFFAIR; TO, DIRECTIONS, SWIMMING-POOL; and FREQUENT, FOREST-FIRES. These count only if the event they describe is in the recall. The plain words say which event that is. The same detail attached to a different event does not count.
